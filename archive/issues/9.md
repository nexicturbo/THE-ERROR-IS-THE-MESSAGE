> Attogram: Tell me a bit more about the premier league in general. Assume I know nothing about it.

Kimi:

Think of the Premier League as England's top-tier professional soccer league — essentially the highest level of the sport in the country, and arguably the most-watched sports league on the planet.

Here's how it works from the ground up:

The Basics
- 20 teams compete each season.
- Each team plays every other team twice — once at home, once away — for a total of 38 games per team.
- 3 points for a win, 1 point for a draw, 0 for a loss.
- The team with the most points at the end wins the title. If points are tied, it goes to goal difference (goals scored minus goals conceded), then goals scored.

Promotion and Relegation
This is the dramatic part. The bottom three teams at the end of the season drop down to the Championship (the second tier). Meanwhile, the top two teams from the Championship automatically come up, and a third joins via playoffs. This means no team is safe — even a giant can fall, and a small club can dream.

The "Big Six" and the Hierarchy
While 20 teams compete, there's a fairly established pecking order:
- Manchester City, Arsenal, Liverpool, Chelsea, Manchester United, Tottenham — these are the "Big Six," with bigger budgets, global fanbases, and expectations of finishing in the top spots.
- Below them, clubs like Newcastle, Aston Villa, Brighton, Everton (your other team today) fight for "European places."
- Then there's a middle tier (where Bournemouth usually lives) just trying to stay up and maybe overachieve.
- The bottom few are in a dogfight to avoid relegation.

European Qualification
The prize for finishing high isn't just pride — it's entry into European competitions:
- Top 4 qualify for the Champions League (the biggest club competition in the world).
- 5th place usually goes to the Europa League.
- 6th and 7th (or cup winners) can qualify for the Conference League.

History
The Premier League was created in 1992 when the top clubs broke away from England's traditional Football League to negotiate their own lucrative TV deals. It transformed English football from a declining, rough-around-the-edges sport into a global entertainment product.

Before 1992, the same competition existed but was called the First Division. Since the rebrand, Manchester United dominated the 1990s and 2000s, but Manchester City has been the powerhouse of the last decade.

Money and Global Reach
The Premier League is broadcast in 188 countries to roughly 4.7 billion people. TV rights deals are enormous — even the smallest club in the league earns more from broadcasting than champions of many other European leagues. This attracts the world's best players and managers.

The Season Calendar
A season runs from August to May. There are no winter breaks (unlike most European leagues), so the schedule is relentless — games every weekend, plus midweek fixtures, plus domestic cup competitions (the FA Cup and the League Cup).

Today
You're watching a game on August 31, 2026 — so this is very early in the 2026–27 season. Teams have only played a few games, so the table is still settling. Bournemouth beating Everton 1-0 at this stage could be an early sign of how both teams' seasons might go.

Kimi:

The Premier League is English. The Netherlands has its own completely separate top division called the Eredivisie — that's where Ajax plays. They're not in the same league system at all.

Think of it like this: the Premier League is England's national competition, and the Eredivisie is yours. The two leagues are connected mainly through player transfers, money, and the fact that many Dutch football fans follow both.

Ajax vs. the Premier League

Ajax is historically one of the greatest clubs in Europe — they've won the European Cup (now Champions League) four times, including three in a row in the early 1970s with Johan Cruyff. But financially, they operate in a completely different universe from Premier League clubs.

To give you a sense of the gap: Chelsea's squad is valued at roughly €1.14 billion, while Ajax's entire squad is valued at about €196 million.  That's nearly a six-to-one difference. Even mid-table Premier League clubs like Bournemouth or Everton have bigger budgets than Ajax.

The Dutch Pipeline to England

Because of that money gap, the Premier League has become the natural next step for Ajax's best players. It's almost a conveyor belt: a star emerges at Ajax, dominates the Eredivisie for a season or two, then gets sold to a Premier League club for tens of millions.

Right now, there are dozens of Dutch players in the Premier League. In fact, the Netherlands' 2026 World Cup squad had 15 players from Premier League clubs — more than half the team.  Some of the biggest names:

- Virgil van Dijk — Liverpool captain, arguably the best defender in the world
- Cody Gakpo — Liverpool forward
- Ryan Gravenberch — Liverpool midfielder (another Ajax product)
- Nathan Aké — Manchester City defender
- Jurriën Timber — Arsenal defender
- Matthijs de Ligt — Manchester United defender (came through Ajax)
- Justin Kluivert — Bournemouth forward (son of Ajax legend Patrick Kluivert) 

So in the very game you're watching right now, Bournemouth have a Dutch player on their books who is literally Ajax royalty.

Why This Matters for Dutch Football

The Eredivisie is essentially a development league now. Ajax, PSV, and Feyenoord produce extraordinary young talent, but they can't afford to keep it. The Premier League's TV money is so enormous that even its relegation-threatened clubs can outbid Ajax for players.

This is why many Dutch fans end up following the Premier League closely — it's where their national team's best players actually play week-to-week. When you watch the Premier League, you're often watching the finished product of the Dutch youth development system.

In short: Ajax is your local giant, but it lives in a different financial and competitive world from the Premier League. The two are connected by a one-way street of talent flowing from Amsterdam to England, driven by money.
